# Numerical Composition

---

## 1. Dataset and Neighboring Datasets

Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote the domain of a single data record. For example, an element $x \in \mathcal{X}$ represents the data associated with one individual. We define the comprehensive data space $\mathcal{D}$ as:

$$
\mathcal{D} = \bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} \mathcal{X}^n
$$

where $\mathcal{X}^n$ denotes the set of datasets containing exactly $n$ records. A dataset $D \in \mathcal{D}$ may contain any finite number of records. For example, a dataset of size $n$ is written as:

$$
D = (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in \mathcal{X}^n
$$

where each $x_i \in \mathcal{X}$ represents one record. 

Two datasets $D, D' \in \mathcal{D}$ are considered *neighboring datasets* if they differ in the contribution of a single record. The exact mathematical meaning depends on the chosen adjacency relation:

**Replace-one Adjacency (Bounded DP):**
Under the replace-one adjacency relation, $D$ and $D'$ have the exact same size and differ in at most one record. That is, for some dataset size $n$,

$$
D = (x_1, \ldots, x_n), \qquad D' = (x'_1, \ldots, x'_n)
$$

with $x_i = x'_i$ for all but at most one index $i$.

**Add/remove-one Adjacency (Unbounded DP):**
Alternatively, under the add/remove-one adjacency relation, $D'$ is obtained from $D$ by adding or removing a single record. Consequently, neighboring datasets may differ in size by one. For example, if $D \in \mathcal{X}^n$, then an add/remove-one neighbor $D'$ may belong to either $\mathcal{X}^{n-1}$ or $\mathcal{X}^{n+1}$.

**Remark:** 
The add/remove-one adjacency relation is particularly natural for algorithms involving *Poisson subsampling*, where each record is independently included with a certain probability, naturally making the resulting dataset size a random variable.

## 2. Differential Privacy

**Definition 1 ($(\varepsilon)$-Differential Privacy (DP))**

Let $M : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{Y}$ be a randomized mechanism, where $\mathcal{Y}$ is the output space. For every pair of neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$, and every measurable set $S\subseteq \mathcal{Y}$, a randomized mechanism \(M\) satisfies $\varepsilon$-differential privacy (DP) if

$$ \Pr[M(D)\in S] \le e^\varepsilon \Pr[M(D')\in S].$$

**Remark.** Since the neighboring relation is symmetric, the same definition can be applied with \(D\) and \(D'\) exchanged:

$$ \Pr[M(D')\in S] \le e^\varepsilon \Pr[M(D)\in S]. $$

**Definition 2 ($(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP)**

Let $M:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Y}$ be a randomized mechanism. For $\varepsilon\ge 0$ and $\delta\in[0,1]$, the mechanism $M$ satisfies $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differential privacy, also called approximate differential privacy, if for every pair of neighboring datasets $D,D'\in\mathcal{D}$ and every measurable set $S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$,
$$\Pr[M(D)\in S] \le e^\varepsilon \Pr[M(D')\in S] + \delta.$$

**Remark.** The parameter $\varepsilon$ controls the multiplicative difference between the output probabilities induced by neighboring datasets, while $\delta$ allows an additional additive slack beyond the pure-DP bound. Smaller values of both $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ correspond to stronger privacy guarantees. When $\delta=0$, $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP reduces to pure $\varepsilon$-DP.

## 3. Output Distributions

Fix two neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$. Since the mechanism $M$ is randomized, $M(D)$ and $M(D')$ are random variables.

Let $P=\mathcal{L}(M(D)),~Q=\mathcal{L}(M(D')),$ where $\mathcal{L}(X)$ denotes the probability distribution, or *law*, of a random variable $X$. Both $P$ and $Q$ are probability distributions on the output space $\mathcal{Y}$.

For a discrete output space, the probability mass at a given output $y\in\mathcal{Y}$ is $P(y)=\Pr[M(D)=y], ~Q(y)=\Pr[M(D')=y].$

When $M$ satisfies pure $\varepsilon$-DP, the two output distributions have the same support. The DP inequalities imply

$$P(y)=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q(y)=0.$$

Consequently, the likelihood ratio $P(y)/Q(y)$ is well defined whenever either distribution assigns positive probability to $y$. When sums are written over the entire output space $\mathcal{Y}$, outputs satisfying $P(y)=Q(y)=0$ are understood to contribute zero. In particular, expressions of the form

$$P(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}$$

are taken to be zero when $P(y)=Q(y)=0$.

For a continuous output space, assuming the distributions admit probability density functions $p$ and $q$ with respect to a common dominating measure $\mu$, the probability of an event $S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$ is given by 

$$P(S) = \int_S p(y)\,d\mu(y), \qquad Q(S) = \int_S q(y)\,d\mu(y).$$

More generally, when $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $Q$, their likelihood ratio is represented by the Radon–Nikodym derivative
$$\frac{dP}{dQ},$$
which is defined $Q$-almost everywhere. If $P$ and $Q$ have densities $p$ and $q$ with respect to $\mu$, then
$$\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) = \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}$$
for $\mu$-almost every $y$ such that $q(y)>0$.

## 4. Privacy Loss

**Definition 3 (Privacy Loss Function)**

Let $P$ and $Q$ denote the output distributions corresponding to two neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$, respectively. Assuming $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $Q$, the privacy loss function from $P$ to $Q$ is defined as:

$$
L_{P \parallel Q}(y) = \log \frac{dP}{dQ}(y)
$$

where $\frac{dP}{dQ}$ denotes the Radon–Nikodym derivative of $P$ with respect to $Q$. This definition is understood to hold $Q$-almost everywhere.

In the discrete setting, the Radon–Nikodym derivative reduces to the ratio of probability masses, so:

$$
L_{P \parallel Q}(y) = \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}
$$

for every $y$ in the common support of $P$ and $Q$.

For continuous output distributions with densities $p$ and $q$ with respect to a common dominating measure $\mu$, we have:

$$
\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) = \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}
$$

for $\mu$-almost every $y$ such that $q(y)>0$. Therefore, the privacy loss function can be equivalently written as:

$$
L_{P \parallel Q}(y) = \log\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}
$$

almost everywhere.

**Remark:** The privacy loss function measures the relative likelihood of an output under $P$ compared with $Q$. In the discrete setting, if $L_{P \parallel Q}(y) > 0$, then $y$ has greater probability under $P$ than under $Q$, whereas if $L_{P \parallel Q}(y) < 0$, then $y$ has greater probability under $Q$ than under $P$. The analogous interpretation holds in the continuous setting in terms of probability densities.


If $P$ and $Q$ are mutually absolutely continuous, reversing the direction of comparison gives:

$$
L_{Q \parallel P}(y) = \log\frac{dQ}{dP}(y) = -L_{P \parallel Q}(y)
$$

almost everywhere. In the discrete setting, this becomes:

$$
L_{Q \parallel P}(y) = \log\frac{Q(y)}{P(y)} = -L_{P \parallel Q}(y)
$$

and for continuous distributions with densities $p$ and $q$, it takes the form:

$$
L_{Q \parallel P}(y) = \log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)} = -L_{P \parallel Q}(y)
$$

almost everywhere.

**Definition 4 (Privacy Loss Random Variable)**

Let $Y \sim P$ denote the random variable representing the output of the mechanism $M$ applied to dataset $D$. The privacy loss random variable from $P$ to $Q$ is defined by evaluating the privacy loss function at the random output $Y$:

$$
L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) = \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(Y), \qquad \text{with} \quad Y \sim P
$$

Similarly, the reverse privacy loss random variable is defined as:

$$
L_{Q \parallel P}(Y') = \log\frac{dQ}{dP}(Y'), \qquad \text{with} \quad Y' \sim Q
$$

## 5. Expected Privacy Loss and KL Divergence

**Lemma 1 (Expected Privacy Loss)**

Let $Y\sim P$. Then the expected privacy loss from $P$ to $Q$ is equal to the Kullback-Leibler divergence from $P$ to $Q$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q).$$

**Proof**

By Definition 4,
$$L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) = \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(Y), ~Y\sim P.$$

Therefore, taking the expectation with respect to $P$,

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(y)\,dP(y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q). $$

In the discrete setting, this reduces to:

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = \sum_{y\in \mathcal{Y}} P(y) \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q).$$

For continuous output distributions with densities $p$ and $q$ with respect to a common dominating measure $\mu$, the same identity becomes:

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = \int_{\mathcal{Y}} p(y) \log\frac{p(y)}{q(y)} \,d\mu(y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q),$$

where the density ratio is understood almost everywhere on the set where $q(y)>0$. $\square$

**Remark.** KL divergence is directional and is generally not symmetric:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q) \neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\parallel P).$$

Correspondingly,

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q).$$

If instead $Y'\sim Q$, then evaluating the same privacy loss function $L_{P\parallel Q}$ gives:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{Y'\sim Q} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y') \right] = \mathbb{E}_{Y'\sim Q} \left[ \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(Y') \right] = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\parallel P). $$

Equivalently, using the reverse privacy loss function,

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y'\sim Q} \left[ L_{Q\parallel P}(Y') \right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\parallel P).$$

## 6. Bounding the Privacy Loss

**Lemma 2 (Worst-Case Privacy Loss under $\varepsilon$-DP)**

Suppose a randomized mechanism $M$ satisfies pure $\varepsilon$-DP. Let 

$$
P = \mathcal{L}(M(D)), \qquad Q = \mathcal{L}(M(D'))
$$

be the output distributions corresponding to two neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$. Then the privacy loss from $P$ to $Q$ is bounded in magnitude by $\varepsilon$.

In the discrete setting,

$$
\left|L_{P \parallel Q}(y)\right| = \left| \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} \right| \le \varepsilon
$$

for every output $y$ in the common support of $P$ and $Q$.

In the general setting,

$$
\left|L_{P \parallel Q}(y)\right| = \left| \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) \right| \le \varepsilon
$$

almost everywhere.

**Proof**

We consider the discrete and general cases separately.

**Discrete Case**

Since $M$ satisfies $\varepsilon$-DP, for every measurable set $S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$:

$$
P(S) \le e^\varepsilon Q(S) \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q(S) \le e^\varepsilon P(S)
$$

For a discrete output $y$, take $S = \{y\}$. Then:

$$
P(y) \le e^\varepsilon Q(y) \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q(y) \le e^\varepsilon P(y)
$$


For every $y$ in the common support of $P$ and $Q$, these inequalities imply:

$$
e^{-\varepsilon} \le \frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} \le e^\varepsilon
$$

Taking natural logarithms yields:

$$
-\varepsilon \le \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} \le \varepsilon
$$

Therefore, we establish the bound:

$$
\left|L_{P \parallel Q}(y)\right| \le \varepsilon
$$

for every output $y$ in the common support. Moreover, pure $\varepsilon$-DP implies that $P(y) = 0$ if and only if $Q(y) = 0$, ensuring that $P$ and $Q$ have the exact same support.

**General Case**

For a general output space, the differential privacy inequalities

$$
P(S) \le e^\varepsilon Q(S) \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q(S) \le e^\varepsilon P(S)
$$

hold for every measurable set $S \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$. These inequalities imply that $P$ and $Q$ are mutually absolutely continuous. Therefore, the Radon–Nikodym derivatives

$$
\frac{dP}{dQ} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{dQ}{dP}
$$

exist almost everywhere. 

Let $r(y) = \frac{dP}{dQ}(y)$. We first show that $r(y) \le e^\varepsilon$ for $Q$-almost every $y$. 

Suppose that the measurable set $`A = \{ y : r(y) > e^\varepsilon \}`$ has positive $Q$-measure. Then, evaluating the probability of $A$ under $P$ gives:

$$
P(A) = \int_A r(y)\,dQ(y) > e^\varepsilon Q(A)
$$

which contradicts the $\varepsilon$-DP inequality $P(A) \le e^\varepsilon Q(A)$. Therefore,

$$
\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) \le e^\varepsilon
$$

for $Q$-almost every $y$. 

Similarly, applying the reverse DP inequality gives:

$$
\frac{dQ}{dP}(y) \le e^\varepsilon
$$

almost everywhere. 

Since $\frac{dQ}{dP}(y) = \left( \frac{dP}{dQ}(y) \right)^{-1}$ almost everywhere, we obtain:

$$
\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) \ge e^{-\varepsilon}
$$

Thus, combining both bounds gives:

$$
e^{-\varepsilon} \le \frac{dP}{dQ}(y) \le e^\varepsilon
$$

almost everywhere. 

Taking natural logarithms gives $-\varepsilon \le \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(y) \le \varepsilon$, and hence we obtain our final bound:

$$
\left|L_{P \parallel Q}(y)\right| \le \varepsilon
$$

almost everywhere.

$\square$

**Remark.** 
Lemma 2 shows that a pure $\varepsilon$-DP mechanism bounds the privacy loss by $\varepsilon$ almost surely. Consequently, $\left|L_{P \parallel Q}(Y)\right| \le \varepsilon$ holds $P$-almost surely. Taking expectations and applying Lemma 1 establishes the naive worst-case bound:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = \mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] \le \varepsilon
$$

## 7. Bounding the Expected Privacy Loss

For clarity, the derivations in this section are presented in the discrete setting. The corresponding results extend to general output distributions by replacing sums with the appropriate integrals and probability-mass ratios with Radon–Nikodym derivatives.

**Lemma 3 (Symmetrized KL Divergence)**

The sum of the two directed KL divergences can be written as:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \bigl(P(y)-Q(y)\bigr) \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}
$$

**Proof**

By definition,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}
$$

and

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} Q(y)\log\frac{Q(y)}{P(y)}
$$

Since $\log\frac{Q(y)}{P(y)} = -\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}$, we obtain:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} - \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} Q(y)\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \bigl(P(y)-Q(y)\bigr) \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}
$$

$\square$

**Lemma 4 (Bounding KL Divergence by $\ell_1$ Distance)**

Suppose $P$ and $Q$ are the output distributions induced by an $\varepsilon$-DP mechanism on two neighboring datasets. Then:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \le \varepsilon \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)|
$$

**Proof**

By Lemma 3,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \bigl(P(y)-Q(y)\bigr) \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}
$$

For every $y$ in the common support of $P$ and $Q$, the two factors $P(y)-Q(y)$ and $\log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)}$ always have the same sign. Therefore,

$$
\bigl(P(y)-Q(y)\bigr) \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} = |P(y)-Q(y)| \left| \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} \right|
$$

By Lemma 2, pure $\varepsilon$-DP implies $\left| \log\frac{P(y)}{Q(y)} \right| \le \varepsilon$. Hence,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) \le \varepsilon \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)|
$$

Since KL divergence is non-negative, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \parallel P) \ge 0$, and therefore:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \le \varepsilon \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)|
$$

$\square$

**Lemma 5 (Distance Between $\varepsilon$-DP Output Distributions)**

Let $P$ and $Q$ be the output distributions induced by an $\varepsilon$-DP mechanism on two neighboring datasets. Then:

$$
\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)| \le e^\varepsilon-1
$$

**Proof**

Consider first an output $y$ such that $P(y) \ge Q(y)$. By $\varepsilon$-DP, $P(y) \le e^\varepsilon Q(y)$, and therefore:

$$
P(y)-Q(y) \le (e^\varepsilon-1)Q(y)
$$

Since $P(y) \ge Q(y)$, we have $Q(y) = \min\{P(y),Q(y)\}$, so:

$$
|P(y)-Q(y)| \le (e^\varepsilon-1) \min\{P(y),Q(y)\}
$$

Similarly, if $Q(y) > P(y)$, the reverse DP inequality gives $Q(y) \le e^\varepsilon P(y)$, and hence:

$$
Q(y)-P(y) \le (e^\varepsilon-1)P(y)
$$

In this case, $P(y) = \min\{P(y),Q(y)\}$, so again:

$$
|P(y)-Q(y)| \le (e^\varepsilon-1) \min\{P(y),Q(y)\}
$$

Thus, for every $y$,

$$
|P(y)-Q(y)| \le (e^\varepsilon-1) \min\{P(y),Q(y)\}
$$

Summing over $y \in \mathcal{Y}$,

$$
\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)| \le (e^\varepsilon-1) \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \min\{P(y),Q(y)\}
$$

Since $\min\{P(y),Q(y)\} \le P(y)$ for every $y$,

$$
\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \min\{P(y),Q(y)\} \le \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(y) = 1
$$

Therefore,

$$
\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)| \le e^\varepsilon-1
$$

$\square$

**Remark:** 
The bound in Lemma 5 is sufficient for establishing the $O(\varepsilon^2)$ behavior of the expected privacy loss, although sharper bounds on the distance between $P$ and $Q$ are possible.

**Theorem 1 (Bound on Expected Privacy Loss)**

Let $P$ and $Q$ be the output distributions induced by an $\varepsilon$-DP mechanism on two neighboring datasets. Then the expected privacy loss from $P$ to $Q$ satisfies:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] \le \varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1)
$$

In particular, in the small-$\varepsilon$ regime,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] = O(\varepsilon^2)
$$

**Proof**

Combining Lemma 4 and Lemma 5 gives:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q) \le \varepsilon \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} |P(y)-Q(y)| \le \varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1)
$$

By Lemma 1, $`\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \parallel Q)`$. Therefore,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] \le \varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1)
$$

To examine the behavior for small $\varepsilon$, we use the Taylor expansion $e^\varepsilon = 1+\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} + O(\varepsilon^3)$, which gives:

$$
e^\varepsilon-1 = \varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2} + O(\varepsilon^3)
$$

Multiplying by $\varepsilon$,

$$
\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1) = \varepsilon^2 + \frac{\varepsilon^3}{2} + O(\varepsilon^4)
$$

Therefore, as $\varepsilon \to 0$,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] = O(\varepsilon^2)
$$

$\square$

**Remark:** 
The quadratic bound is most informative in the small $\varepsilon$ regime. In particular, $\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1) = \varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^3)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$, which is substantially smaller than the naive bound $`\mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ L_{P \parallel Q}(Y) \right] \le \varepsilon`$ when $\varepsilon$ is sufficiently small. 

For larger values of $\varepsilon$, however, the bound $\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1)$ may exceed the simpler bound $\varepsilon$. In that regime, the naive worst-case bound may therefore provide the tighter guarantee. 

The significance of the $O(\varepsilon^2)$ behavior is that, in repeated applications of differentially private mechanisms, the expected privacy-loss contribution of each mechanism can be much smaller than its worst-case magnitude. This distinction between the typical drift of the privacy loss and its worst-case bound is one of the key ideas underlying refined composition analyses.

## 8. Connection to the Central Model of Differential Privacy

The mathematical framework developed in the previous sections describes the central differential privacy (CDP).

**The Central Model**

In the central model, a trusted data curator collects the raw data of individuals and constructs a dataset $D$. The curator then applies a randomized mechanism $M$ to the dataset and releases only the randomized output $Y = M(D).$

An analyst or adversary observes the released output $Y$, but does not directly observe the underlying dataset. Differential privacy guarantees that the distribution of the released output does not change substantially when one individual's contribution to the dataset is modified.

This is in contrast to Local Differential Privacy (LDP), where each individual randomizes their own data before sending it to an untrusted server or aggregator.

**The Role of the Privacy Loss Random Variable**

For two neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$, let

$$P = \mathcal{L}(M(D)), \qquad Q = \mathcal{L}(M(D')).$$

The privacy loss random variable $L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) = \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(Y),~Y\sim P,$ provides a useful way to quantify how distinguishable the two output distributions are.

Pure $\varepsilon$-DP guarantees the almost-sure bound $|L_{P\parallel Q}(Y)| \le \varepsilon,$ while Theorem 1 shows that, in the small-$\varepsilon$ regime, $`\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = O(\varepsilon^2).`$

This distinction between the worst-case magnitude of the privacy loss and its expected value plays an important role in refined privacy accounting.

**Composition over Multiple Mechanisms**
In practice, a curator often applies several differentially private mechanisms to the same underlying dataset. Suppose $k$ mechanisms are applied sequentially, possibly adaptively, so that the $i$-th mechanism may depend on the outputs released in the previous rounds.

In the adaptive setting, the privacy loss at round $i$ is defined conditionally on the previous transcript. If $T_{i-1}$ denotes all outputs released before round $i$, then the privacy loss increment can be written schematically as
$$L_i = \log \frac{ P_i(Y_i\mid T_{i-1}) }{ Q_i(Y_i\mid T_{i-1}) }.$$

The total privacy loss across the $k$ rounds is then
$$L_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} L_i.$$

If each mechanism satisfies $\varepsilon$-DP, the basic composition theorem gives the worst-case bound
$$|L_{\mathrm{total}}| \le k\varepsilon,$$
corresponding to $k\varepsilon$-DP.

However, this worst-case bound does not capture the typical behavior of the accumulated privacy loss. For small $\varepsilon$, each privacy-loss increment has magnitude at most $O(\varepsilon)$, while its expected value is only $O(\varepsilon^2)$.

As a result, refined composition analyses separate the cumulative privacy loss into a fluctuation term and a drift term. A typical advanced-composition bound has the form
$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}} = O\left( \sqrt{k\log(1/\delta')}\,\varepsilon + k\varepsilon^2 \right),$$
for an additional failure probability $\delta'>0$.

More concretely, a standard form of the advanced composition theorem gives
$$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{total}} = \sqrt{2k\log(1/\delta')}\,\varepsilon + k\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1).$$

For small $\varepsilon$,
$$\varepsilon(e^\varepsilon-1) = O(\varepsilon^2),$$
so the second term corresponds to the cumulative drift of the privacy loss, while the first term captures its fluctuations around that drift.

Thus, the $O(\varepsilon^2)$ expected privacy loss established in Theorem 1 helps explain why refined composition can provide substantially tighter guarantees than the naive $k\varepsilon$ bound.

**Moments Accountant and Rényi Differential Privacy**
When a mechanism is composed many times, controlling only the expected privacy loss is generally not sufficient. Strong privacy guarantees require information about the tails of the privacy-loss distribution.

A natural way to capture this information is through the exponential moments of the privacy loss. For example, one may study the cumulant-generating function $`\log \mathbb{E}_{Y \sim P} \left[ e^{\lambda L_{P \parallel Q}(Y)} \right]`$.

Bounding such quantities provides information not only about the mean of the privacy loss, but also about its higher-order fluctuations and tail behavior.

This idea underlies accounting techniques such as the Moments Accountant and is closely related to Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP). Rather than tracking only a single worst-case privacy-loss bound, these methods control exponential moments or Rényi divergences, which compose particularly cleanly across repeated applications.

Such accounting methods are especially useful in applications such as DP-SGD (Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent), where thousands of noisy gradient updates may need to be composed while maintaining a useful overall privacy guarantee.

The privacy loss random variable therefore provides the bridge between the basic definition of DP and more refined privacy-accounting frameworks based on concentration, moments, and Rényi divergence.

## 9. Connection to Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy ($\rho$-zCDP)

The behavior of the privacy loss random variable developed in the previous sections motivates stronger privacy-accounting frameworks that control not only its worst-case magnitude, but also its higher-order fluctuations. One such framework is zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy (zCDP).

While pure $\varepsilon$-DP guarantees the almost-sure bound

$$|L_{P\parallel Q}(Y)| \le \varepsilon,$$

zCDP controls the exponential moments of the privacy loss. This concentration behavior is conveniently expressed through Rényi divergence.

**Definition 5 (Rényi Divergence)**
Let $P$ and $Q$ be probability distributions such that $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $Q$. For $\alpha\in(1,\infty)$, the Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$ from $P$ to $Q$ is defined as

$$D_\alpha(P\parallel Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1} \log \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim Q} \left[ \left( \frac{dP}{dQ}(Y) \right)^\alpha \right].$$

Using the privacy loss function

$$L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) = \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(Y), \qquad Y\sim P,$$

the same quantity can equivalently be written as

$$D_\alpha(P\parallel Q) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1} \log \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ e^{(\alpha-1)L_{P\parallel Q}(Y)} \right].$$

As $\alpha\to1$, Rényi divergence converges to KL divergence:

$$\lim_{\alpha\to1} D_\alpha(P\parallel Q) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q) = \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right].$$

**Definition 6 ($\rho$-Zero-Concentrated Differential Privacy)**
A randomized mechanism $M$ satisfies $\rho$-zCDP if, for every pair of neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$, and for every $\alpha\in(1,\infty)$,

$$D_\alpha(P\parallel Q) \le \rho\alpha,$$
where

$$P=\mathcal{L}(M(D)), \qquad Q=\mathcal{L}(M(D')).$$

Equivalently, setting $\lambda=\alpha-1>0$,

$$\log \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ e^{\lambda L_{P\parallel Q}(Y)} \right] \le \rho\lambda(\lambda+1).$$

Thus, zCDP controls the exponential moments of the privacy loss rather than only its worst-case magnitude.

**Relationship with Expected Privacy Loss**
Taking the limit $\alpha\to1$ in the zCDP condition gives
$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q) \le \rho.$$

By Lemma 1,

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\parallel Q),$$

and therefore every $\rho$-zCDP mechanism satisfies

$$\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right] \le \rho.$$

Therefore, the zCDP parameter $\rho$ directly bounds the expected privacy loss while simultaneously controlling its higher exponential moments.

**Composition under zCDP**
One of the main advantages of zCDP is its simple composition rule. If mechanisms $M_1,\ldots,M_k$ satisfy

$$\rho_1\text{-zCDP}, \ldots, \rho_k\text{-zCDP},$$

respectively, then their adaptive composition satisfies

$$\left( \sum_{i=1}^k\rho_i \right) \text{-zCDP}.$$

In particular, if every mechanism satisfies $\rho$-zCDP, then the composition satisfies

$$k\rho\text{-zCDP}.$$

Thus, zCDP converts the statistical concentration of the privacy loss into a privacy parameter that composes additively. This makes it particularly useful for analyzing mechanisms that are applied repeatedly, and it provides a natural bridge between privacy-loss random variables, Rényi divergence, and modern privacy accounting.

## 10. Privacy Loss Distributions and Composition

**Definition 7 (Privacy Loss Distribution)**

Let $P$ and $Q$ denote the output distributions of a randomized mechanism $M$ on two neighboring datasets $D$ and $D'$, respectively:

$$P=\mathcal{L}(M(D)), \qquad Q=\mathcal{L}(M(D'))$$

Recall that the privacy loss random variable from $P$ to $Q$ is:

$$L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) = \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(Y), \qquad Y\sim P$$

The Privacy Loss Distribution (PLD) from $P$ to $Q$ is the probability distribution of $L_{P\parallel Q}(Y), Y\sim P$:

$$\omega_{P\parallel Q} = \mathcal{L}\left( L_{P\parallel Q}(Y) \right), \qquad Y\sim P$$

**Remark.** The privacy loss function maps each possible mechanism output $y$ to a privacy-loss value $L_{P\parallel Q}(y)$. Since the mechanism output $Y$ is random, evaluating the privacy loss function at $Y$ produces the privacy loss random variable: $Z = L_{P\parallel Q}(Y).$ The PLD is the probability distribution of this random variable $Z$. The PLD is directional. In general, $\omega_{P\parallel Q} \neq \omega_{Q\parallel P}$.

**Definition 8 (Privacy Profile)**

Let $M:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{Y}$ be a randomized mechanism. For each pair of neighboring datasets $D\sim D'$, let:

$$P=\mathcal{L}(M(D)), \qquad Q=\mathcal{L}(M(D'))$$

denote the corresponding output distributions.

For a fixed ordered pair $P$ and $Q$, and for $\varepsilon\ge0$, define the directional privacy profile from $P$ to $Q$ as:

$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = \sup_{S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}} \left( P(S)-e^\varepsilon Q(S) \right),$$

where the supremum is taken over all measurable sets $S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$. In other words, $\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon)$ is the smallest non-negative value $\delta$ such that:

$$P(S) \le e^\varepsilon Q(S)+\delta$$

holds for every measurable set $S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}$.

The privacy profile of the mechanism $M$ is then defined by taking the worst case over all neighboring datasets $D$, and $D'$:

$$\delta_M(\varepsilon) = \sup_{D, D'} \delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon).$$

Since the neighboring relation is symmetric, the supremum over $D$ and $D'$ accounts for both directions of comparison.

**Remark.** The quantity $\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon)$ is also known as the hockey-stick divergence between $P$ and $Q$ with parameter $e^\varepsilon$. By Definition 2, $M$ satisfies $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP if and only if $\delta_M(\varepsilon)\le\delta$. For a fixed value of $\varepsilon$, the privacy profile gives the smallest additive slack required for the mechanism to satisfy $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP.

**Lemma 6 (Computing the Directional Privacy Profile from the PLD)**

Let $P$ and $Q$ be mutually absolutely continuous probability distributions, and let:

$$Z=L_{P\parallel Q}(Y), \qquad Y\sim P.$$

Let $\omega_{P\parallel Q}$ denote the corresponding privacy loss distribution. The directional privacy profile can be computed by:

$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = \mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\omega_{P\parallel Q}} \left[ \left( 1-e^{\varepsilon-Z} \right)_+ \right] $$

where $(x)_+=\max\{x,0\}$.

**Proof**

By Definition 8,
$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = \sup_{S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}} \left( P(S)-e^\varepsilon Q(S) \right).$$

The maximizing region consists of outputs for which $\frac{dP}{dQ}(y)>e^\varepsilon$, or equivalently, $L_{P\parallel Q}(y)>\varepsilon$.

Define:

$$
A_\varepsilon = \lbrace y : L_{P \parallel Q}(y) > \varepsilon \rbrace
$$

Then:

$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = P(A_\varepsilon) - e^\varepsilon Q(A_\varepsilon).$$

Since $L_{P\parallel Q}(y) = \log\frac{dP}{dQ}(y)$, we have:

$$\frac{dQ}{dP}(y) = e^{-L_{P\parallel Q}(y)}.$$

Therefore,

$$Q(A_\varepsilon) = \int_{A_\varepsilon} e^{-L_{P\parallel Q}(y)} \,dP(y).$$

Substituting this expression gives:

$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = \int_{A_\varepsilon}1\,dP(y) - \int_{A_\varepsilon} e^{\varepsilon-L_{P\parallel Q}(y)} \,dP(y) = \int_{A_\varepsilon} \left( 1-e^{\varepsilon-L_{P\parallel Q}(y)} \right) \,dP(y).$$

The integrand is positive when $L_{P\parallel Q}(y)>\varepsilon$. 

Therefore,

$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = \mathbb{E}_{Y\sim P} \left[ \left( 1-e^{\varepsilon-L_{P\parallel Q}(Y)} \right)_+ \right].$$

Since $Z=L_{P\parallel Q}(Y)$ has distribution $\omega_{P\parallel Q}$, we obtain:

$$\delta_{P\parallel Q}(\varepsilon) = \mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\omega_{P\parallel Q}} \left[ \left( 1-e^{\varepsilon-Z} \right)_+ \right].$$

$\square$

**Proposition 1 (Composition of Privacy Loss Distributions)**

Consider two non-adaptive randomized mechanisms whose randomness is independent. Let $(P_1,Q_1)$ and $(P_2,Q_2)$ denote the corresponding pairs of output distributions under two neighboring datasets.

Let $Y_1\sim P_1$ and $Y_2\sim P_2$, and and define the privacy loss random variables:

$$Z_1 = \log\frac{dP_1}{dQ_1}(Y_1) \qquad \text{and} \qquad Z_2 = \log\frac{dP_2}{dQ_2}(Y_2).$$

Let $\omega_1=\mathcal{L}(Z_1)$ and $\omega_2=\mathcal{L}(Z_2)$ denote their corresponding privacy loss distributions.

Then the privacy loss random variable of the composed mechanism satisfies:

$$Z_{\mathrm{total}}=Z_1+Z_2,$$ 

and its privacy loss distribution is given by the convolution:

$$\omega_{\mathrm{total}} = \omega_1 * \omega_2.$$

**Proof**

Since the mechanisms are non-adaptive and use independent randomness, the joint output distributions under the two neighboring datasets are:

$$P=P_1\otimes P_2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad Q=Q_1\otimes Q_2.$$

The privacy loss random variable of the joint mechanism is therefore:

$$Z_{\mathrm{total}} = \log \frac{ d(P_1\otimes P_2) }{ d(Q_1\otimes Q_2) } (Y_1,Y_2).$$

Using the product structure of the joint distributions, 

$$\frac{ d(P_1\otimes P_2) }{ d(Q_1\otimes Q_2) } (Y_1,Y_2) = \frac{dP_1}{dQ_1}(Y_1) \frac{dP_2}{dQ_2}(Y_2).$$

Therefore,

$$ Z_{\mathrm{total}} = \log \left[ \frac{dP_1}{dQ_1}(Y_1) \frac{dP_2}{dQ_2}(Y_2) \right] = \log\frac{dP_1}{dQ_1}(Y_1) + \log\frac{dP_2}{dQ_2}(Y_2) = Z_1+Z_2.$$

Since $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent random variables, the distribution of their sum is the convolution of their individual distributions. 

Consequently,

$$\omega_{\mathrm{total}} = \mathcal{L}(Z_{\mathrm{total}}) = \mathcal{L}(Z_1+Z_2) = \omega_1*\omega_2.$$

$\square$

**Corollary 1 ($k$-Fold Composition)**

Consider $k$ non-adaptive mechanisms with independent randomness, and let $Z_i = L_{P_i\parallel Q_i}(Y_i), \quad Y_i\sim P_i$ denote the privacy loss random variable of the $i$-th mechanism.

Let $\omega_i=\mathcal{L}(Z_i)$ be its privacy loss distribution.

Then the total privacy loss is:

$$Z_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} Z_i,$$

and the PLD of the composed mechanism is:

$$\omega_{\mathrm{total}} = \omega_1*\omega_2*\cdots*\omega_k.$$

If the same mechanism, with PLD $\omega$, is independently composed $k$ times, then:

$$\omega_{\mathrm{total}} = \omega^{*k},$$

where $\omega^{*k}$ denotes the $k$-fold convolution of $\omega$.

**Remark.** The result above relies on the product structure arising from non-adaptive composition with independent randomness. In an adaptive composition, the total privacy loss still decomposes additively through conditional likelihood ratios. However, the distribution of each privacy-loss increment may depend on the previous transcript. Consequently, in general, the PLD of a general adaptive composition cannot be obtained by simply convolving fixed unconditional PLDs.

## 11. Numerical PLD Composition

For many mechanisms, the exact PLD is continuous or has a sufficiently complicated support that direct symbolic composition is impractical.

A numerical PLD accountant therefore approximates the privacy loss distribution on a finite grid. Conceptually, the accounting procedure consists of the following transformations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{single-step mechanism} \\
&\quad\downarrow \\
&\text{single-step PLD} \\
&\quad\downarrow \\
&\text{composition of PLDs} \\
&\quad\downarrow \\
&\text{composed PLD} \\
&\quad\downarrow \\
&\delta(\varepsilon)\text{ privacy profile}
\end{aligned}
$$

Suppose a discretized PLD is represented on privacy-loss values $z_1,\ldots,z_m$ with associated probability masses $w_1,\ldots,w_m$. 

For a given $\varepsilon$, the privacy profile can then be approximated by:

$$\delta(\varepsilon) \approx \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j \left( 1-e^{\varepsilon-z_j} \right)_+ $$

Repeated composition requires repeated convolution of these discrete distributions. Direct convolution can become computationally expensive when the number of compositions or grid points is large.

For this reason, practical numerical PLD accountants often use algorithms based on the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Since convolution in the original domain corresponds to multiplication in the Fourier domain, many compositions can be evaluated more efficiently.

For $k$ compositions of the same PLD, schematically:

$$\omega^{*k} = \mathcal{F}^{-1} \left[ \left( \mathcal{F}(\omega) \right)^k \right]$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the discrete Fourier transform.

The resulting composed PLD can then be used to evaluate the privacy profile for different values of $\varepsilon$.
